Q&A Preparation

The brief's four Q&A areas, with prepared answers

Guiding principle: Green Turf doesn't want dramatic change. The existing pipeline of mandates gets delivered first. Anything new is added only where it helps that delivery, and strategic change waits until the pipeline is complete. I've written this from outside the business, so I'd test it and adjust it in the first 30 days.

Likely questions, grouped under the four areas the brief says the Q&A will cover. Each answer comes back to the same principle: deliver the existing mandates first, and add complexity only where it helps.

Your reasoning

Why put delivery of the existing pipeline ahead of improvement?

Because it's work we've already won. The revenue, the client relationships and our reputation are already on the line. You've been clear that you want the pipeline completed before any strategic change, and I agree. Changing things mid-delivery risks distracting the people doing the work. Completing it well also gives me the evidence to recommend the right changes afterwards.

If you could only do one thing in 90 days, what would it be?

Make sure every committed job finishes well: on time, safely and at the margin it was quoted at. If the safety check on live sites turns up a gap, that comes first.

Why that order?

Delivery first, then margin and cash, then a light weekly rhythm, and the next stage last. Each step only goes ahead if it supports the one before it.

Your assumptions

What assumptions have you made?

That there's a committed pipeline of mandates to deliver, that the business wants stability rather than dramatic change, and that some information sits with individuals and in spreadsheets. I'd test each of these in the first 30 days and adjust the plan at the day-30 review with Mick and Dean.

What if the numbers you need don't exist?

In the first 90 days I'd work with what exists and ask the people running the jobs. I'd note the gaps for the day-90 recommendations rather than build new reporting while the team is delivering.

How you handle challenge or ambiguity

We don't want dramatic change. How does your plan reflect that?

It's the basis of the whole plan. Level 1 is delivering what's already been won. Level 2 is a few light controls, using the tools you already have, and only where they help delivery. Level 3 covers systems, structure and growth planning, and I'd prepare it as options rather than implement it in 90 days. You decide what happens next, and when. It's also how I've worked before: at SILK I stabilised the Victorian operations first, and the acquisition and integration work came after that.

What if Mick disagrees with your priorities at day 30?

That's what the day-30 review is for. I'd want to understand what he's seeing that I'm not, test it against what I've found on the live jobs, and adjust. It's his business. My job is to give him the clearest picture I can and a recommendation, then commit to the decision.

How would you handle a long-serving supervisor who's wary of you?

By helping them deliver their jobs first: clearing what's in their way without changing how they work. Their knowledge is exactly what I need, and trust comes from following through.

Dean wins a large new job that would stretch the current crews. What do you do?

Welcome it, then do a quick capacity check with Dean and Mick before we commit to dates: crews, plant, subcontractors and the impact on jobs already promised. We agree a start date that protects existing clients, or agree what we'd need to bring in. Everything can be delivered; it's a question of cost and timing.

How you adapt to new information

What would make you change this plan?

A committed job in trouble, a safety gap or a cash issue. Any of those goes straight to the top. Or Mick and Dean telling me a Level 3 item is more urgent than I think, in which case we'd agree how to do it without disrupting delivery.

How will we know you've succeeded at 90 days?

The committed pipeline is delivered or on an agreed path, safely and at quoted margin. Crews and the office haven't been disrupted. Milestones are invoiced as they're completed. And there's an agreed list of next-stage options, with the timing left to you.

Where does technology fit?

Later, and only behind the process. The prototypes are examples of what could be considered once the pipeline is delivered. Nothing new would be introduced in the first 90 days.

For a question I haven't prepared for: what I'd want to understand first, what I'd do, and how I'd know it worked. Then check the answer against the principle: does this help us deliver what we've already committed to?
